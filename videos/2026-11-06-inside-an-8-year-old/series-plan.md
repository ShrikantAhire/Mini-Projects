"8 year old" itself is barely searched. People search the feelings (friends, attention, comparison, confidence), not the age. So the age goes in the series name and the feelings go in the episode titles.

**What breaks out right now (vidIQ outliers, last 12 months):**
- Story videos about a child being left out beat their channel averages by far: "Koko Wasn't Invited to the Birthday Party" (102x its channel's normal views), "Didn't Get Picked... and It Broke Him" (1M views), "He Was Alone at Lunch... Until This Happened" (44,500 views on a 2,000-subscriber channel), "New Kids First Day at School" (88x).
- Almost everything else about children's emotions is aimed at preschoolers: songs, flashcards, "match the face". Very little explains what a 7-10-year-old is going through, with real research, for kids and parents together.

**The gap:** a story-led series for ages 7-10 that a child enjoys as a story and a parent or teacher can trust as psychology. Nobody in the results does both.

## The psychology the series rests on (all checked)

| Idea | Source | What it says |
|---|---|---|
| Comparing yourself to others starts here | Ruble, Boggiano, Feldman & Loebl (1980), Developmental Psychology | Children under about 7-8 barely use comparison with peers to judge themselves. From around then, it becomes a main way they judge themselves. |
| The self turns from "I'm great at everything" to "I'm good at some things" | Harter (1999), The Construction of the Self | Young children's self-views are inflated. In middle childhood they become realistic, split by area (school, sport, looks, friends), and many children's self-esteem dips. |
| Appearance worries start early | Dohnt & Tiggemann (2006), Journal of Youth and Adolescence | Many girls aged 6 already wanted a thinner body. Peers mattered more than media. |
| Being left out hurts most for self-esteem at 8-9 | Abrams et al. (2011), British Journal of Developmental Psychology | In a ball-toss game (Cyberball), being excluded hit 8-9-year-olds' self-esteem harder than it hit teenagers' or adults'. |
| Friendship runs on fairness at this age | Selman's friendship stages | Roughly 7-12: friends are people who are fair and take turns, and friendships break over "that's not fair". |
| Belonging is a basic need | Baumeister & Leary (1995), Psychological Bulletin | People need to belong; being shut out affects mood, thinking and health. |
| School age is about feeling capable | Erikson (1950), Childhood and Society | Ages 6-12: industry versus inferiority. Children build a sense of "I can do things", or of "I'm not good enough". |

**One line I'd change from your brief:** "getting attention is sometimes more important than life itself". The research supports "belonging feels as important as food or safety to an 8-year-old's brain". Saying it's more important than life could alarm parents and isn't what the studies show. The series shows how big it feels from the inside, and the narrator says plainly that it feels that way.

## The series

**Working title:** Inside an 8-Year-Old's Mind
**Main character:** Om, now 8. He's the same boy from the first video, two years older. Returning viewers meet him again, and new viewers lose nothing.
**New characters:** Kabir (Om's best friend, who drifts toward the popular group), Maya (a classmate who worries about how she looks), Ms. Rao (class teacher), Mom and Dad, and the "Club" (three kids who decide who's in).
**Narrator:** Vestal (the American voice from the first video).

**Every episode uses the same 4-part shape**, so kids know what's coming and adults get their part:
1. **Story (about 5 minutes):** Om's day, told as a story with suspense. This is the part kids rewatch.
2. **Inside Om's head:** his thoughts as bubbles, plus a recurring "feelings meter" on his chest that rises and falls. This becomes the series' signature visual.
3. **What science says (about 2 minutes):** one or two studies, told as mini stories, with research cards like the last video.
4. **For parents and teachers (about 1.5 minutes):** 2-3 things to do. Then a cliffhanger that leads into the next episode.

### Episode 1: "Am I Good Enough?" (comparison, appearance, the self)
- **Story:** Om turns 8. On class photo day he notices he's the shortest in the row. Test papers come back, and for the first time he looks at everyone else's score before his own. Maya won't smile in the photo because of her teeth.
- **Science:** Ruble (comparison switches on around 7-8), Harter (the self gets "realistic"), Dohnt & Tiggemann (appearance worries start early), Erikson (feeling capable).
- **For parents:** compare a child with who they were last month, not with classmates. Praise specific effort. Don't joke about a child's looks, even kindly.
- **Cliffhanger:** at lunch, Kabir sits with three kids who've started a "club". There's no space left at the table. *"Next episode: the Club."*

### Episode 2: "The Club" (friends, groups, attention)
- **Story:** Om tries to get into the club. He does a loud stunt in class to be noticed. It works for one day, then it backfires. Kabir has to choose between Om and the club, and picks the club.
- **Science:** Abrams' Cyberball study (being left out at 8-9), Selman (fairness friendships), Baumeister & Leary (belonging), plus why attention-seeking is usually a bid to belong, not "bad behavior".
- **For parents and teachers:** respond to the need behind the attention-seeking. One real friend matters more than group status. Teachers can rotate groups so no child is always the one left over.
- **Cliffhanger:** Om gets home. Mom asks "How was school?" Om says "Fine." *"Next episode: what Om doesn't tell his parents."*

### Episode 3: "Fine" (parents, home, being seen)
- **Story:** what Om thinks about his parents. He doesn't want to worry them, Dad is often on his phone, and Om believes they'd be disappointed. One evening, a parent notices and really listens. Om tries again at school with one person instead of a group. Om finds out that being seen by one person is enough to start.
- **Science:** parents as a secure base (Ainsworth), and emotion coaching (Gottman), which means naming the feeling before fixing the problem. Phone interruptions and children's behavior: I'll verify the exact study before using it.
- **For parents:** a 10-minute daily "no-phone" check-in. Ask "What was the best and hardest part of today?" instead of "How was school?" Notice the child's effort before their results.
- **Series ending:** Om at the park, eight years old, with one friend beside him. It calls back to the first video.

## What makes it engaging for each audience
- **Kids:** one hero they recognize, a mystery in every episode, cliffhangers, funny side characters, and the feelings meter. They can root for Om.
- **Parents:** they see the inside of their child's day, and every episode ends with things to try that evening.
- **Teachers and psychologists:** every claim has a named, real source on screen and in the description. Nothing is diagnosed. There's a "talk to a professional" note where it fits.

## Release plan
Episode 1 goes up, then Episode 2 one week later and Episode 3 one week after that. Each episode ends with "Episode 2 is next week" and an end screen pointing to the series playlist. Cut 2-3 Shorts from each episode, the same way we cut Shorts from the first video. Make one thumbnail style for the whole series, with "EP 1/2/3" on each.

## Decision needed: who the videos are "for" on YouTube
YouTube treats videos marked "made for kids" differently: comments, the notification bell, end screens and cards are all switched off. Those are exactly the tools a "come back for Episode 2" plan depends on.
- **Recommended:** write the narration to parents and families ("your child", "when you were 8"), so kids watch it as a story with a parent, and mark the videos "not made for kids". Comments, notifications, end screens and the series flow all keep working.
- **Alternative:** write it directly to kids and mark it "made for kids". It might reach more children, but comments, notifications and end screens are off, so the episode-to-episode pull is weaker.
- YouTube can review the setting itself. Animated child characters are fine for a family video, as long as the voice and framing are clearly for families.
