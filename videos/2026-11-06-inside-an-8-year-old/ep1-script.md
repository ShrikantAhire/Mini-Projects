# Episode 1: "Am I Good Enough?" | Inside an 8-Year-Old's Mind

**Length target:** about 10 minutes
**Audience:** families. Kids watch it as a story, and parents and teachers get the "why" and the "what to do". YouTube setting: not made for kids.
**Characters:**
- **Leo:** 8, our hero. Amber T-shirt, hair that sticks up at the back, one missing front tooth.
- **Mason:** Leo's best friend since kindergarten. Grew over the summer. Blue cap.
- **Maya:** classmate who's worried about her teeth.
- **Ethan, Ava and Noah:** the kids who start "the Club" at the end.
- **Ms. Carter:** class teacher.
- **Mom and Dad**

**Recurring visual:** the feelings meter on Leo's chest. It's introduced in this episode and used in all three.

---

## [0:00] COLD OPEN

**VO:**
Leo turned eight on a Tuesday.
He woke up early, ran to the bathroom mirror, and checked whether he looked any older.
He didn't look any different, with the same hair sticking up at the back and the same gap where his front tooth used to be.
But something had changed, and it wasn't anything Leo could see in a mirror. It was happening inside his head.

This is Inside an 8-Year-Old's Mind.
Over three episodes, we'll follow Leo through one ordinary week of school, and we'll see it the way he sees it.
If you have a child around this age, you'll recognize a lot of it. If you teach eight-year-olds, you'll recognize the whole classroom. And if you were ever eight yourself, this story is partly yours.

Episode one: Am I good enough?

**VISUAL:** Leo jumps out of bed and runs to the mirror, checks his face, height and tooth gap. A slow push-in on his head, which glows faintly. Title card: "Inside an 8-Year-Old's Mind", then "Episode 1: Am I Good Enough?"

## [1:00] THE FEELINGS METER

**VO:**
Before we follow Leo to school, let's give him something we'll use through this whole series: a feelings meter, right here on his chest.
When Leo feels good about himself, the needle goes up. When he feels small, it drops.
On his birthday morning, with pancakes and a card from Grandma, it's nearly full.
Keep an eye on it. By the end of this week, it's going to move a lot.

**VISUAL:** A gauge appears on Leo's chest and the needle swings up. Kitchen: pancakes with a candle, Mom and Dad singing, a card. The needle settles near full.

## [1:30] PHOTO DAY

**VO:**
Wednesday is class photo day.
Ms. Carter lines everyone up by height, shortest at the front.
Leo has never really thought about his height before. Today he's standing at the very end of the front row, and he knows exactly why he's there.
Behind him, in the back row, is Mason, his best friend since kindergarten. Mason grew over the summer. Leo didn't.
The photographer says, "Big smiles, everybody!"
Leo smiles. But inside, a small voice is busy counting. Shortest. End of the row. Front, where everyone can see.

Two places down from Leo, Maya isn't smiling at all.
She's pressing her lips together, tight.
Last week, a boy in class said her front teeth looked like a rabbit's. Everyone laughed. For them, the joke was over in two seconds.
For Maya, it isn't over. She's checked her teeth in every mirror since.
"Come on, show me those teeth!" says the photographer.
Maya keeps her mouth shut.
Click.

**VISUAL:** Class lined up by height. Leo at the front end, Mason tall in the back row. A thought bubble over Leo: "Shortest." Maya with her lips pressed shut, and a quick flashback of a boy pointing and kids laughing. Camera flash. Leo's meter dips.

## [3:00] THE TEST

**VO:**
On Thursday, Ms. Carter hands back the spelling tests.
Leo got seven out of ten.
Last year, Leo would have looked at his paper, seen the seven and thought, "Seven! Nice."
This year he does something he's never done before. Before he even looks at his own score, he looks at Mason's.
Mason got ten.
Then he looks at the girl next to him. Nine.
Now Leo's seven looks different. It's the same paper with the same number on it, but it feels smaller.
The needle on his meter drops again.

At recess, Ethan does a perfect cartwheel and everybody claps. Leo can't do a cartwheel.
Ava draws a horse that actually looks like a horse. Leo's horses always come out looking like dogs.
By lunchtime, Leo has made a list in his head. He's not the tallest. He's not the fastest. He's not the best at spelling. He's not the best at drawing.
Nobody asked him to make that list. Nobody even knows it exists. He made it all on his own.

**VISUAL:** Test papers handed out. Leo's paper shows 7/10, and his eyes slide sideways to Mason's 10/10 and a neighbor's 9/10. The 7 shrinks on the page. At recess, Ethan cartwheels to applause, and Ava holds up a great horse drawing next to Leo's dog-horse. A list writes itself in a thought bubble with four lines, each crossed out. Meter drops.

## [4:30] HOME

**VO:**
That evening, Mom asks, "How was school?"
Leo says, "Fine."
Then, in the bath, out of nowhere, he asks, "Mom, am I short?"
Mom laughs and says, "You're perfect, sweetheart."
Leo doesn't believe her. Moms have to say that.

Later, lying in bed, Leo goes over his list again. Shortest. Seven out of ten. No cartwheel. Horses that look like dogs.
He tries to think of one thing he's the best at. He thinks for a long time.
Then he remembers: he's the best at making Mason laugh. Nobody makes Mason laugh like Leo does.
That one is true. He falls asleep holding on to it.

If you're a parent, this moment might feel familiar. The question comes sideways, at bedtime or in the car, never at the dinner table. And the answer we give, kindly and quickly, often doesn't land.
We'll come back to why in a minute.

**VISUAL:** Dinner table, Leo says "Fine" with a flat face. Bath time, with bubbles, as Leo asks his question. Mom's warm laugh and a heart over her. Leo's eyes roll slightly and his meter doesn't move. Bedroom at night: Leo in bed, his list floating above him. Then a warm memory bubble of Mason laughing, the needle lifts a little, and Leo falls asleep smiling.

## [5:15] WHAT SCIENCE SAYS

**VO:**
What's happening to Leo is completely normal, and psychologists have studied it for decades.

In 1980, psychologist Diane Ruble and her colleagues looked at how children judge their own work.
They found that younger children mostly judge themselves by their own results. "I did it" is enough.
From around seven or eight, that starts to change. Children begin judging themselves by looking at the kids around them.
Leo didn't get worse at spelling this week. He started comparing.

Psychologist Susan Harter spent her career studying how children see themselves.
Ask a five-year-old if they're the fastest runner in the class, and you'll often get a big, confident yes.
In middle childhood, that picture becomes more realistic. Children start sorting themselves into areas: school, sports, looks and friends. And many children's self-esteem dips during these years, as they work out where they stand.
That's Leo's list.
And it isn't all bad news. Seeing yourself more realistically is a real step forward. It's how children find out what they want to get better at. The hard part is that nobody warns them it's coming.

Looks are part of it, and earlier than most adults expect.
In 2006, Australian researchers Hayley Dohnt and Marika Tiggemann studied girls aged five to eight.
By age six, many of them already wanted a thinner body.
The strongest influence wasn't TV or magazines. It was what they thought other girls their age felt about their bodies.
For Maya, one joke from a classmate mattered more than anything she'd ever seen on a screen.

Back in 1950, psychologist Erik Erikson described this whole stage of life.
Between about six and twelve, he said, children are working on one big question: "Am I capable?"
Kids who mostly get the answer "yes" build a feeling that they can do things.
Kids who keep getting "no" can start to believe they just aren't good enough.
That's why the meter on Leo's chest matters so much right now.

## [7:45] FOR PARENTS AND TEACHERS

**VO:**
So what can the adults around Leo do? Here are three things you can start tonight.

One: help your child compare themselves with themselves. "Last month you got five out of ten. This week you got seven. That's your brain growing." It gives your child a comparison they can actually win.

Two: be specific. "You're perfect" slides right off an eight-year-old, because they know it isn't true. "You kept practicing that word until you got it" sticks, because it's true, and it's something they can do again tomorrow.

Three: no jokes about a child's body. Not about height, teeth, weight or hair. Not even the kind ones. At this age, a joke can last much longer than the laugh.

And for teachers: small routines can turn an ordinary day into a ranking. Lining kids up by height, or reading scores out loud, tells every child exactly where they stand. Lining up by birthday instead takes the ranking away.

And if you're eight and watching this: making a list like Leo's doesn't mean the list is true. Everyone has a list like that. Even grown-ups.

**VISUAL:** Three numbered cards like in the first video:

Friday at lunch, Leo carries his tray to his usual spot, next to Mason.

There are four seats at that table, and all four are taken.
Mason looks up and sees Leo, holding his tray. For a second, neither of them knows what to do.
Leo's meter drops further than it has all week.
