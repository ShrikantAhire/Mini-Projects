The usual advice is willpower. Want it more. Stop making excuses. Push through.

In 2012, Wilhelm Hofmann, Roy Baumeister and their colleagues put that to the test. For a week, 205 adults in Germany carried phones that buzzed at random times. Every buzz, they reported what they wanted and whether they were resisting it.

If discipline were about willpower, the people highest in self-control should have been resisting the most. They resisted the least. Their desires were weaker, and they felt less inner conflict.

They simply ran into fewer temptations to begin with. Gritting your teeth, the skill you've practised for years, is something they barely use.

Quick question. What's one thing you keep dragging yourself to start? You'll need it at the end.

In 1992, psychologist Robert Eisenberger described something called learned industriousness.

Effort shows up in your body. Your shoulders tighten before you open the file, and you suddenly need water or a snack. Anything your body feels can be conditioned, the same way a smell can come to mean home.

Eisenberger found that when effort keeps getting followed by reward, the feeling of effort starts to carry some of that reward. And it spreads. Rewarding hard work on one task made people try harder on other tasks too.

People even value things because they were hard. Nobody runs a marathon because it's the quickest way to cover 42 kilometres.

So liking effort isn't a personality you're born with. It's a learned link. Some people picked it up by accident. You can build it on purpose.

In the 1990s, Wolfram Schultz recorded dopamine cells in monkeys learning that a light meant juice was coming. At first, the cells fired when the juice arrived. Once the monkeys learned the pattern, the burst moved to the light.

The good feeling shifts from the reward to whatever reliably comes before it.

That's why your phone pulls at you the moment you see it, before a single notification.

Now pause and answer this. What does opening that file, or that gym bag, usually lead to? For a lot of us it's a blank page and a voice saying we should be further along by now.

So your brain flinches. That flinch is a forecast, and forecasts can be changed.

Think about what got you praise growing up. The report card. The trophy. The acceptance letter. People celebrated the outcome. The weeks of boring work behind it went unmentioned, or you heard, "You should have started earlier."

So your brain learned that effort is the bill, and the reward is something you might get after paying it. Effort never got paired with anything good.

If school came easily to you, it can be worse. In 1998, Claudia Mueller and Carol Dweck praised some children for being smart and others for working hard. When the kids picked their next task, more of the "smart" kids chose the easy one.

When you're praised for not needing effort, needing it feels like a bad sign. So when something gets hard, not trying can feel safer than struggling where people can see.

Then there's how your forced sessions end. You finish the workout and compare it to last year. You close the report and list everything you didn't get to.

The last thing your brain files next to effort is self-punishment. Every session like that is a rep that teaches effort leads to feeling bad. That's why the people who've tried the hardest often find it hardest to start.

Pushing harder only adds more of those reps. The goal is to change what starting predicts. Five fixes, each one you can set up tonight.

One. Pay the effort, not the outcome. Results are slow, so they're poor teachers. The moment a session ends, before you judge it, mark it. A tick on a paper calendar, or "I showed up" said out loud. Every time, even after a bad day.

Tonight: put a paper calendar where you work, with a pen next to it.

It'll feel like a medal for a bad session. That's fine. You're training the link first.

Two. Put the reward at the door. In 2014, Katherine Milkman's team at Wharton gave people gripping audiobooks they could only listen to at the gym. Early on, that group went about 51 percent more often. The boost faded after a holiday break, so use this to get through the door in the first few weeks.

Tonight: pick one thing you'll only have during the hard task. A specific coffee, a playlist, a podcast. If you can have it anytime, it stops pointing to the task.

Three. Shrink the entry until saying no feels silly. "Write the chapter" triggers the flinch. "Open the document and write one sentence" doesn't.

In 2010, Phillippa Lally's team at University College London found new habits took about 66 days on average to become automatic, and missing one day didn't break the progress.

Tonight: write down the two-minute version of your task. A small version you do every day beats a big one you do twice.

Four. Design the room, not your resolve. In a 2015 study, students with more self-control were more likely to pick a quiet room over a distracting one before the work even began.

A decision you make on a calm Sunday saves you a fight on a tired Thursday night.

Tonight: phone charger in another room. Gym bag in the car, not by the couch.

Five. Never end on failure. In 1993, Daniel Kahneman's team had people hold a hand in painfully cold water for 60 seconds. In another trial, they added 30 seconds of slightly warmer water. Most people chose to repeat the longer one, because the ending shaped the memory.

So don't stop at the worst moment. If your last set failed, do one easy set after it. Tonight, decide your last easy rep, the small thing you'll always finish on.

Now take the task you picked earlier. Tonight, rate from one to ten how much you dread starting it.

The task won't get lighter. A five-kilometre run is still five kilometres. What changes is the debate you have with yourself before you start. Over time, effort starts to feel familiar, the way regular runners feel off on days they skip.
