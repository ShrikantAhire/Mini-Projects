[0:25-0:50] THE LOOP
VO: Around noon, the guilt shows up. You watch a video about discipline. You write a new plan in neater handwriting than the last one, and for three days it works.
VO: On day four it falls apart, and you're back to the same question. What is wrong with me?
VISUAL: Stickman at desk at noon, guilt cloud overhead. Writes a plan. Calendar flips: day 1 tick, day 2 tick, day 3 tick, day 4 the page tears. Question mark grows over his head.

[0:50-1:20] PROMISE AND OPEN LOOP
VO: Nothing is wrong with you. Your brain learned a lesson about effort, and it learned it very well.
VO: In the next twelve minutes you'll see where that lesson came from, the research on how to rewrite it, and five changes you can start tonight. At the end there's a seven-day test you can run on yourself.
VO: Save this video now. Day four is when most plans break, and the last two minutes are written for that day.
VISUAL: The question mark turns into a lightbulb. Three cards slide in: "Where it came from", "How to rewrite it", "7-day test". A bookmark icon drops onto the screen with "Day 4".

[1:20-2:30] PART 1: DISCIPLINED PEOPLE BARELY FIGHT
VO: The usual advice is willpower. Want it more. Stop making excuses. Push through.
VO: In 2012, Wilhelm Hofmann, Roy Baumeister and their colleagues put that to the test. They gave 205 adults in Germany phones that buzzed at random times for a week. Every buzz, people reported what they wanted right then, and whether they were trying to resist it. Nearly 8,000 reports came in.
VO: If discipline were about willpower, the people highest in self-control should have been resisting the most. They resisted the least. Their desires were weaker, and they felt less inner conflict.
VO: They weren't winning more fights. They were getting into fewer of them. So gritting your teeth, the skill you've practised for years, is something disciplined people barely use.
VISUAL: A drill sergeant stickman yells "PUSH!". Cut to a lab: phones buzzing on 205 tiny stickmen. A counter climbs to 7,827. Two stickmen side by side: one wrestling a giant donut, the other walking past a closed bakery door, calm.

[2:30-2:45] QUICK CHECK
VO: Quick question. When did you last feel like you were dragging yourself to start something? Keep that example in mind. You'll use it in the test at the end.
VISUAL: Screen freezes. Text: "Pick your task." Stickman holds up a blank card that fills in with "gym? writing? studying?"

[2:45-4:00] PART 2: EFFORT IS A FEELING YOU CAN TRAIN
VO: In 1992, a psychologist named Robert Eisenberger published a theory he called learned industriousness.
VO: His starting point is simple. Effort has a feel in your body. A heaviness in your chest before a hard task. Tight eyes. A pull toward the door. And anything you can feel can be conditioned, the same way a smell can come to mean home.
VO: His research showed that when effort keeps getting followed by reward, the feeling of effort starts to carry some of that reward. The heaviness starts to signal that something good is coming. And it spreads. Rewarding hard work on one task made people try harder on completely different tasks later.
VO: In 2018, Michael Inzlicht at the University of Toronto and two colleagues published a paper called The Effort Paradox. Their point: effort is a cost, and people also value things because they were hard. There are helicopters, and people still climb mountains. People even value furniture more when they had to build it themselves.
VO: So liking effort isn't a personality you're born with. It's a learned link. Some people picked it up by accident. You can build it on purpose.
VISUAL: Stickman's chest glows grey and heavy as he faces a task. A plus sign and a gold coin appear after each effort; the grey glow slowly turns gold. The gold spreads to other icons: gym, books, laptop. Helicopter flies past a stickman climbing a mountain, smiling. Stickman proudly sits on a wobbly flat-pack shelf.

[4:00-5:15] PART 3: DOPAMINE MOVES TO THE SIGNAL
VO: You've heard dopamine is the reward chemical. That's only half right, and the other half is where your leverage is.
VO: In the 1990s, Wolfram Schultz recorded dopamine cells in monkeys learning that a light meant juice was coming. At first, the cells fired when the juice arrived. As the monkeys learned the pattern, the burst moved. It fired at the light. When the juice then arrived exactly as expected, the cells barely reacted. And when expected juice didn't come, activity dropped below normal.
VO: The signal moves backwards in time. It leaves the reward and attaches to whatever predicts the reward.
VO: That's why picking up your phone feels like relief before you've even unlocked it. Your phone predicts something good, every time.
VO: Now pause for three seconds and answer this. What does opening your laptop for the hard task predict? For most people the answer is strain, self-criticism, and finding out how far behind they are.
VO: So your brain flinches. The flinch is a prediction, and predictions can be updated.
VISUAL: Monkey, light bulb, juice cup. A spark sits on the juice, then slides backwards along an arrow to the light. Cut to stickman reaching for phone with a spark already at his fingertips. Freeze frame, countdown 3, 2, 1. A laptop opens and three grey clouds come out labelled "strain", "you're behind", "not good enough". Stickman flinches.

[5:15-6:30] PART 4: WHERE THE PREDICTION CAME FROM
VO: Think about what got you praise growing up. The A on the report card. The trophy. The acceptance letter. People celebrated the outcome.
VO: The three weeks of boring work that produced it went unmentioned. Or you heard, "You should have started earlier."
VO: So your nervous system learned that effort is a toll. You pay it at the gate, and the reward waits on the other side. Under that setup, effort never gets paired with anything good, which is the exact opposite of what Eisenberger showed you need.
VO: If school came easily to you, it can be worse. In 1998, Claudia Mueller and Carol Dweck praised one group of children for being smart and another for working hard. Then they let them choose the next task. More of the "smart" kids picked the easy one. The "hard-working" kids were more likely to pick the challenge.
VO: When you're praised for not needing effort, effort starts to look like evidence that you're not as smart as people said. So the moment something gets hard, you close the laptop before anyone notices.
VISUAL: Young stickman on a stage with a trophy, crowd cheering. Rewind: the same kid alone at a desk for weeks, no one watching. A toll booth labelled "EFFORT" with the prize on the far side. Split screen: one kid gets a "SMART!" sticker and picks a tiny easy puzzle; another gets a "HARD WORKER!" sticker and picks a big one.

[6:30-7:30] PART 5: HOW FORCING MAKES IT WORSE
VO: Now think about how your forced sessions end. You drag yourself through a workout or two hours of work, and you finish with, "That was awful. I barely got anything done. Tomorrow I need to do twice this."
VO: The last thing your brain files next to effort is self-punishment. Every forced, self-critical session is a rep that teaches effort leads to feeling bad about yourself.
VO: That's why people who've tried the hardest often feel the most resistance to starting. Their brains learned the lesson well.
VO: More force means more reps of the wrong lesson. And you can't decide to enjoy a feeling, any more than you can decide to like a smell. Motivation won't do it either. Motivation is weather.
VO: The goal is to change what starting predicts.
VISUAL: Stickman finishes a workout and a storm cloud of insults rains on him. A filing cabinet drawer labelled "EFFORT" gets a folder stamped "FEEL BAD". The drawer fills up. A weather icon flips from sun to rain over a stickman named "Motivation".

[7:30-8:15] FIX 1: PAY THE EFFORT, NOT THE OUTCOME
VO: Five ways to do that. Each one comes with something you can set up tonight.
VO: One. Pay the effort, not the outcome. Results are slow and mostly outside your control, so they're poor teachers. Attach the reward to the act itself.
VO: The moment a session ends, before you judge it, you mark it. A tick on a paper calendar. Saying "I showed up" out loud. It has to happen within seconds, and it has to happen every time, even on a bad day.
VO: Tonight: put a paper calendar where you work and a pen next to it.
VO: The cost: your ego will say you're rewarding mediocrity. You're not grading performance here. You're pairing a feeling with a reward.
VISUAL: Number "1" stamps onto screen. Stickman finishes, immediately draws a big red X on a wall calendar, says "I showed up" in a speech bubble. A small ego stickman on his shoulder rolls its eyes.

[8:15-9:15] FIX 2: PUT THE REWARD AT THE DOOR
VO: Two. Put the reward at the door. Dopamine moves to the signal, so put something good at the start of the hard thing.
VO: In 2014, Katherine Milkman and her colleagues at Wharton gave people gripping audiobooks they could only listen to at the gym. Early in the study, that group went to the gym about 51 percent more often than the control group.
VO: The effect faded after a holiday break. A much larger follow-up with nearly 7,000 people found a smaller boost, around 10 to 14 percent, but it held up for months.
VO: So this gets you through the door in the first few weeks. It won't carry you for a decade.
VO: Tonight: pick one thing you'll only have during the hard task. A specific coffee, a playlist, a podcast. The rule is that it's exclusive. If you get it anyway, it predicts nothing.
VISUAL: Number "2". A door labelled "GYM" with headphones hanging on the handle. Bar chart jumps up 51 percent, then shrinks after a "Thanksgiving" calendar page. A second, wider bar chart shows a smaller but steady rise. A coffee cup with a padlock that only opens at the desk.

[9:15-10:10] FIX 3: SHRINK THE ENTRY
VO: Three. Shrink the entry until saying no feels silly. Your brain doesn't resist work. It resists how big the work looks. "Write the chapter" triggers the flinch. "Open the document and write one sentence" doesn't.
VO: In 2010, Phillippa Lally's team at University College London tracked 96 people building a new daily habit. On average it took about 66 days to become automatic, with a range from 18 days to 254. And missing a single day didn't knock anyone off track. The people who failed were the ones who quit.
VO: Tonight: write down the two-minute version of your task. That's the whole commitment. If you stop after two minutes, it counts.
VO: The cost: it feels too small to matter. Small is what you can repeat.
VISUAL: Number "3". A huge boulder labelled "WRITE THE CHAPTER" shrinks to a pebble labelled "ONE SENTENCE". A curve rises toward "automatic" at day 66; one dot drops off the curve for a missed day and the line keeps rising. A sticky note: "2 minutes".

[10:10-11:00] FIX 4: DESIGN THE ROOM
VO: Four. Design the room, not your resolve. Remember, the high self-control people in Hofmann's study met fewer temptations.
VO: A 2015 study by Brian Ent, Roy Baumeister and Dianne Tice showed one way they do it. Students with more self-control were more likely to choose a quiet room over a distracting one before the work even began.
VO: Every choice you make while you're calm is a choice your tired 11 p.m. self doesn't have to win.
VO: Tonight: phone charger in another room. Gym clothes on the chair. The app deleted, not resisted.
VO: The cost: you have to plan when you feel fine, which is when you least feel like bothering.
VISUAL: Number "4". Two doors: "QUIET ROOM" and "PARTY ROOM". The disciplined stickman walks into the quiet one. Night scene: tired stickman at 11 p.m. finds the phone is in the kitchen and shrugs. Gym clothes folded on a chair. App icon dragged into a bin.

[11:00-11:55] FIX 5: END ON A WIN
VO: Five. Never end on failure. This one repairs the damage from Part 5.
VO: In 1993, Daniel Kahneman and his colleagues had people hold a hand in painfully cold water for 60 seconds. In another trial, they did the same 60 seconds and then kept the hand in for 30 more while the water warmed slightly. Asked which trial to repeat, most people picked the longer one. The ending shaped the memory more than the total pain.
VO: Your sessions work the same way. So stop ending in the wreckage. Don't finish a workout on the set you failed. Don't close the laptop mid-spiral. End on something small you can finish, then stop while it still feels good.
VO: Tonight: decide your "last easy rep". One easy set, one clean sentence, one small task you always close with.
VISUAL: Number "5". Stickman with hand in an ice bucket, shivering; a thermometer ticks up slightly in the extra 30 seconds and his face relaxes. He points to "repeat this one". Cut to stickman finishing with one easy push-up and a thumbs-up, then closing the laptop with a smile.

[11:55-12:50] THE 7-DAY TEST
VO: Now take the task you picked earlier and run this seven-day test.
VO: Tonight, rate from one to ten how much you dread starting it. Write the number down.
VO: Days one to three: do the two-minute version, mark the calendar, keep the reward exclusive, end on your easy rep.
VO: Day four is when you'll want to skip. Do the two-minute version anyway and mark the X. That's the day this video was saved for, so come back and watch the five fixes again.
VO: Day seven: rate your dread again. You're not looking for zero. You're looking for the number to move.
VO: Put both numbers in the comments. I read them, and I'll pick the most interesting ones for a follow-up video.
VISUAL: A 7-day calendar fills in. A dread meter at "8". Days 1 to 3 get X's. Day 4 flashes red with the bookmark icon from the opening. Day 7: the meter drops to "6". Comment bubble: "Day 1: 8. Day 7: 6."

[12:50-13:25] WHAT ACTUALLY CHANGES
VO: The work doesn't become easy. Hard things stay hard. What fades is the argument. The forty-second negotiation at the alarm. The daily debate about whether you're the kind of person who does this.
VO: Effort starts to feel familiar, like the ache after a workout that you miss when you skip it.
VISUAL: A tiny courtroom inside the stickman's head with a judge and lawyers. The lights switch off and the courtroom empties. Stickman stretches after a workout, smiling at a sore arm.

[13:25-13:55] CLOSE
VO: In 1890, William James wrote that the great thing in all education is to make our nervous system our ally instead of our enemy.
VO: Back to six in the morning. The alarm goes off. Your eyes open in the dark. And this time your feet are on the floor before the argument starts.
