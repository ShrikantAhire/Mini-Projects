The usual advice is willpower. Want it more. Stop making excuses. Push through.

In 2012, Wilhelm Hofmann, Roy Baumeister and their colleagues put that to the test. They gave 205 adults in Germany phones that buzzed at random times for a week. Every buzz, people reported what they wanted right then, and whether they were trying to resist it. Nearly 8,000 reports came in.

If discipline were about willpower, the people highest in self-control should have been resisting the most. They resisted the least. Their desires were weaker, and they felt less inner conflict.

The people with the most self-control simply ran into fewer temptations to begin with. Gritting your teeth, the skill you've practised for years, is something they barely use.

In 1992, a psychologist named Robert Eisenberger published a theory he called learned industriousness.

His starting point is simple. Effort shows up in your body. Your shoulders tighten before you open the file. Your breathing gets shallow. You suddenly need water, a snack, or one quick message. And anything your body feels can be conditioned, the same way a smell can come to mean home.

His research showed that when effort keeps getting followed by reward, the feeling of effort starts to carry some of that reward. The tight shoulders start to signal that something good is on the way. And it spreads. Rewarding hard work on one task made people try harder on completely different tasks later.

In 2018, Michael Inzlicht at the University of Toronto and two colleagues published a paper called The Effort Paradox. Their point: effort is a cost, and people also value things because they were hard. Nobody runs a marathon because it's the quickest way to cover 42 kilometres. People even value furniture more when they had to build it themselves.

So liking effort isn't a personality you're born with. It's a learned link. Some people picked it up by accident. You can build it on purpose.

You've heard dopamine is the reward chemical. That's only half right, and the other half is where your leverage is.

In the 1990s, Wolfram Schultz recorded dopamine cells in monkeys learning that a light meant juice was coming. At first, the cells fired when the juice arrived. As the monkeys learned the pattern, the burst moved. It fired at the light. When the juice then arrived exactly as expected, the cells barely reacted. And when expected juice didn't come, activity dropped below normal.

So the good feeling shifts from the reward to whatever reliably comes before it.

Your phone is the easy example. It pays out often enough that the pull starts the moment you see it on the table, before you've read a single notification.

Now pause for three seconds and answer this. What does opening that file, or that gym bag, usually lead to? For a lot of us it's a blank page, yesterday's mess, and a voice saying we should be further along by now.

So your brain flinches. That flinch is a forecast, and forecasts can be changed.

Think about what got you praise growing up. The A on the report card. The trophy. The acceptance letter. People celebrated the outcome.

The three weeks of boring work that produced it went unmentioned. Or you heard, "You should have started earlier."

So your brain learned that effort is the bill, and the reward is something you might get after paying it. Under that setup, effort never gets paired with anything good, which is the exact opposite of what Eisenberger showed you need.

If school came easily to you, it can be worse. In 1998, Claudia Mueller and Carol Dweck praised one group of children for being smart and another for working hard. Then they let them choose the next task. More of the "smart" kids picked the easy one. The "hard-working" kids were more likely to pick the challenge.

When you're praised for not needing effort, needing it feels like a bad sign. So when something gets hard, not trying can feel safer than trying and struggling where people can see.

Now think about how your forced sessions end. You finish the workout and compare it to how strong you were last year. You close the report and list everything you didn't get to.

The last thing your brain files next to effort is self-punishment. Every forced, self-critical session is a rep that teaches effort leads to feeling bad about yourself.

That's why the people who've tried the hardest often find it hardest to start. They've had the most practice at the wrong lesson.

Pushing harder only adds more of that practice. You can't argue yourself into liking something, and motivation rises and falls with your sleep, your mood and your inbox.

The goal is to change what starting predicts.

Five ways to do that. Each one comes with something you can set up tonight.

One. Pay the effort, not the outcome. Results are slow and mostly outside your control, so they're poor teachers. Attach the reward to the act itself.

The moment a session ends, before you judge it, you mark it. A tick on a paper calendar. Saying "I showed up" out loud. It has to happen within seconds, and it has to happen every time, even on a bad day.

Tonight: put a paper calendar where you work and a pen next to it.

The cost: it'll feel like giving yourself a medal for a bad session. That's fine. Right now you're training the link between effort and reward. Judging the quality can come later.

Two. Put the reward at the door. Dopamine moves to the signal, so put something good at the start of the hard thing.

In 2014, Katherine Milkman and her colleagues at Wharton gave people gripping audiobooks they could only listen to at the gym. Early in the study, that group went to the gym about 51 percent more often than the control group.

The effect faded after a holiday break. A much larger follow-up with nearly 7,000 people found a smaller boost, around 10 to 14 percent, but it held up for months.

So this gets you through the door in the first few weeks. It won't carry you for a decade.

Tonight: pick one thing you'll only have during the hard task. A specific coffee, a playlist, a podcast. The rule is that it's exclusive. If you can have it anytime, it stops pointing to the task.

Three. Shrink the entry until saying no feels silly. Your brain reacts to the size of the job it's picturing. "Write the chapter" triggers the flinch. "Open the document and write one sentence" doesn't.

In 2010, Phillippa Lally's team at University College London tracked 96 people building a new daily habit. On average it took about 66 days to become automatic, with a range from 18 days to 254. And missing a single day didn't knock anyone off track. The people who failed were the ones who quit.

Tonight: write down the two-minute version of your task. That's the whole commitment. If you stop after two minutes, it counts.

The cost: it feels too small to matter. A small version you do every day beats a big one you do twice.

Four. Design the room, not your resolve. Remember, the high self-control people in Hofmann's study met fewer temptations.

A 2015 study by Brian Ent, Roy Baumeister and Dianne Tice showed one way they do it. Students with more self-control were more likely to choose a quiet room over a distracting one before the work even began.

A decision you make on a calm Sunday saves you a fight on a tired Thursday night.

Tonight: phone charger in another room. Gym bag in the car, not by the couch. The app deleted from your phone.

The cost: you have to plan when you feel fine, which is when you least feel like bothering.

Five. Never end on failure. This one repairs the damage from those forced sessions.

In 1993, Daniel Kahneman and his colleagues had people hold a hand in painfully cold water for 60 seconds. In another trial, they did the same 60 seconds and then kept the hand in for 30 more while the water warmed slightly. Asked which trial to repeat, most people picked the longer one. The ending shaped the memory more than the total pain.

Your sessions work the same way. So don't stop at the worst moment. If your last set failed, do one easy set after it. If the writing went badly, finish one short paragraph you're happy with, then close the laptop.

Tonight: decide your "last easy rep". One easy set, one clean sentence, one small task you always close with.

The task doesn't get lighter. A five-kilometre run is still five kilometres. What changes is the talk you have with yourself before you start. The bag by the door stops being a question you answer every evening.

Over time, effort starts to feel like something you know. Regular runners often feel a bit off on the days they don't run.
