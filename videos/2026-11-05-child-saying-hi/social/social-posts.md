## Part 2
**Caption:**
```
Part 2 of 5: Kids think they're less liked than they are.

In 2021, researchers paired up children aged 4 to 11 who had never met. After a short chat, each child said how much they liked the other kid, and how much they thought the other kid liked them.

The 4-year-olds guessed right. From around age 5, children started guessing they were liked less than they really were, and the gap grew as they got older.

So when your child says "nobody likes me," it's often a guess that leans against them. Ask what actually happened, and check the guess against the facts together.

## Part 3
**Caption:**
```
Part 3 of 5: How the fear of rejection makes itself come true.

Psychologist Geraldine Downey called it rejection sensitivity: anxiously expecting rejection, and spotting it in the smallest signals.

In a 1998 study, children in fifth to seventh grade were told a classmate didn't want to join them, with no reason given. The kids who already expected rejection got more upset. A year later, their teachers saw more trouble with friends.

It works like a loop. A child expects rejection and hangs back. The other kids read that as distance and don't invite them. And the child decides, "See? I knew it."

How do parents break the loop? That's Part 4. Missed Parts 1 and 2? They're on our page.

## Part 4
**Caption:**
```
Part 4 of 5: 3 things to do for a shy child.

1. Drop the label. When a child keeps hearing "he's shy," it can become part of who they think they are. Try "He likes to watch for a bit first."

2. Teach "hi" in small steps. Practice three simple sentences at home, then try with one friend before a whole group.

3. Praise the attempt, not the outcome. "You went up and talked to them yourself. I loved that."

Save this for the next playdate. Missed Parts 1 to 3? They're on our page. Part 5 is next, and it's the last one: why a friendly smile can look like teasing to a child.

## Part 5
**Caption:**
```
Part 5 of 5: Why a friendly smile can look like teasing.

A child looks at other kids through their own opinion of themselves. If that mirror is foggy, even a friendly smile can look like teasing.

A young child can't clear that mirror alone. That job starts at home. Psychologist Carl Rogers called it unconditional positive regard: a child feeling loved regardless of their grades, their performance, or how talkative they are.

Kids who feel that kind of love are more willing to go out and explore, because they know there's a safe place to come back to. Attachment researchers call this a secure base.
