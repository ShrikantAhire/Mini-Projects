## Short 1: The liking gap in kids

**Title:** Kids Think They're Less Liked Than They Are
**Length:** ~55s (about 137 words at ~150 words per minute)
**Reuses main scenes:** 41-47 (two kids talking, gauges, age timeline), 48 (Om's thought bubble turning transparent)
**New visuals:** the hook card, the final scene of a parent and Om talking on the sofa

| Time | Beat | VO | VISUAL (9:16) |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0:00-0:04 | Hook | From age five, kids think they're less liked than they are. | Om close-up, worried. A red gauge above his head drops to 5/10. |
| 0:04-0:32 | Name it + proof | In 2021, Wouter Wolf and Michael Tomasello's team tested children aged four to eleven. Pairs of kids who'd never met chatted for a few minutes. Then each one said how much they liked the other kid, and how much they thought the other kid liked them. Four-year-olds showed no gap. From around five, kids guessed they were liked less than they were, and the gap grew with age. | Researcher card "Wolf, Nafe & Tomasello, 2021" at the top. Two kids talking in the middle. Gauges stacked above each kid: "I liked them: 8", "They liked me: 5". The gap glows red. Age timeline runs top to bottom: 4, 5, 11. |
| 0:32-0:47 | Why | Five is roughly when kids start caring what others think. After that, a neutral face can look like a no. So the kid by the bench thinks, "They won't let me in," when the others would often say yes. | Om by the bench. Thought bubble "They won't let me in" covers the kids, then turns transparent and shows them waving. |
| 0:47-0:55 | Takeaway | If your child says nobody likes them, ask what actually happened, and check the guess against the facts together. | Parent and Om on the sofa. A "Guess vs facts" card with two columns. Final frame: Om's gauge rises back to amber 8. |

**Description:** Kids as young as five underestimate how much other kids like them. It's called the liking gap. Full video on the channel. #shorts #psychology #parenting
**Source:** Wolf, Nafe & Tomasello (2021), Psychological Science, 32(5).

## Short 2: The rejection loop

**Title:** The Loop That Keeps Shy Kids on the Sidelines
**Length:** ~55s (about 137 words at ~150 words per minute)
**Reuses main scenes:** 51 and 53-57 (Downey card, invite, the "no", upset gauge, teacher report), 58-62 (the loop and the "NO" sign)
**New visuals:** the hook frame, the final high-five breaking the loop

| Time | Beat | VO | VISUAL (9:16) |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0:00-0:04 | Hook | The fear of rejection can make rejection come true. | Om holding a red "NO" sign facing himself. Freeze frame. |
| 0:04-0:30 | Name it + proof | Psychologist Geraldine Downey calls it rejection sensitivity: anxiously expecting rejection and spotting it everywhere. In a 1998 study, fifth to seventh graders invited a classmate to join them, then heard the classmate didn't want to come, with no reason given. Kids who expected rejection, and expected to feel angry about it, got more upset. A year later, teachers said they had more trouble with peers. | Researcher card "Downey et al., 1998" at the top. Student walks to a door with an invite card. A "?" floats up. Upset gauge rises. Calendar flips, report line falls. |
| 0:30-0:44 | Why | It works like a loop. A child thinks, "They'll reject me," so they hang back or act stiff. The other kids read that as distance and don't invite them. And the child decides, "See? I knew it." | The loop fills the frame, nodes stacked in a circle. Om sits in the centre and shrinks a little on each lap. |
| 0:44-0:55 | Takeaway / loop | Break it at the first step. Praise your child for walking up, even when the answer is no, so a no stops meaning "nobody wants me." | Mom high-fives Om after a "no". The loop cracks and falls apart. Last frame cuts back to Om holding the "NO" sign, so the Short loops to the hook. |

**Description:** Kids who expect rejection often end up rejected, and the loop starts in their own head. Full video on the channel. #shorts #psychology #parenting
**Source:** Downey, Lebolt, Rincón & Freitas (1998), Child Development, 69(4).

## Short 3: Two things to say to a shy child

**Title:** Two Things to Say to a Shy Child
**Length:** ~43s (about 107 words at ~150 words per minute)
**Reuses main scenes:** 10 (the "SHY" label), 32-34 (Kagan card, two babies), 80-81 (label growing and peeled off), 88 (high-five)
**New visuals:** the hook frame, the "1" and "2" number cards

| Time | Beat | VO | VISUAL (9:16) |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0:00-0:03 | Hook | Calling your child shy can make it stick. | A red "SHY" label slaps onto Om's forehead. |
| 0:03-0:17 | Name it + proof | Some kids are cautious with new people from the start. Psychologist Jerome Kagan called it behavioural inhibition: look first, then join. He saw it as temperament, and many of these kids open up with time. | Researcher card "Kagan et al., 1988" at the top. Two babies: one crawls straight to a toy, one looks back first. Text: "Look first, then join." |
| 0:17-0:24 | Why | But when a child keeps hearing "he's shy," it can become part of how they see themselves. | The "SHY" label grows and Om shrinks under it. |
| 0:24-0:43 | Takeaway | So try two things. First, swap the label for "He likes to watch for a bit first." Second, when your child walks up and says hi, praise the attempt, even if the other kids say no. "You went up and talked to them yourself. I loved that." | Card "1": Mom peels the label off, a small amber bubble reads "Likes to watch first". Card "2": Om walks back after a "no", Mom gives him a high-five. Last frame: Om smiling, no label. |

**Description:** The words you use about a cautious child shape how they see themselves. Try these two instead. Full video on the channel. #shorts #parenting #shykids
**Source:** Kagan, Reznick & Snidman (1988), Science, 240.
