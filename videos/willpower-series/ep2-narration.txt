This isn't you. You're the overweight guy who couldn't read. Study all you want. You're going to fail.

He's heard that voice his whole life. He still hears it.

Today is about the voices in your head. Why one harsh voice usually keeps people stuck, what Goggins does differently, and four tools to build a voice that keeps you going.

Let's start with the first voice.

Goggins says the harsh voice in his head isn't lying. It's describing who he was. The kid who stuttered. The guy who lied to be liked. The man who weighed 300 pounds and sat at home. That voice has facts on its side, and that's what makes it so strong.

Most people have a voice like that. It says you always quit. You're lazy. Who are you kidding?

Pause and listen for a second. What does your harsh voice say most often? Notice whether it's describing who you were years ago, or who you are this week.

He didn't win by shutting that voice up. He's never managed that. He won by building a second voice that answers back.

He describes one moment during Navy SEAL training. February, freezing ocean water, arms locked with the men next to him, waves going over his head. The first voice is screaming at him to get out. You're nobody. You've always been nobody.

The second voice says, you're better than that other voice. And he stays in the water.

Over the years, that second voice got stronger. It started in the water, then moved into his studying, his running, the way he eats, the way he shows up as a man. Every small battle it won made it louder next time.

He says most people only ever have one voice. And that one voice comes in two flavours.

The first flavour is the bully. It only insults you. It never helps you.

The second flavour is comfort. It says you've had a hard day, you deserve a treat, start again Monday.

Goggins says when he weighed 300 pounds, being nice to himself meant another box of chocolate donuts and a milkshake from the corner shop. So he decided kindness wasn't for him.

But there's an important difference between comfort and kindness, and research shows it.

In 2012, two researchers, Juliana Breines and Serena Chen, gave students a hard test they were very likely to fail. Afterwards, one group was encouraged to be understanding with themselves about failing. Another group just got a confidence boost about how smart they were.

When the students got the chance to study for a retest, the group that had been understanding with themselves studied longer.

Comfort tells you to stop. Real kindness tells you that you can try again.

A simple test helps. When you mess up, ask what you'd say to a good friend in the same spot. Usually it's honest and kind at the same time. Say that to yourself.

Read that next to Goggins's story and you'll notice something. His second voice isn't soft, but it's on his side. It tells him the truth, and then it tells him he can handle it. That's the voice you want to build.

The next thing he does is unusual. He taught himself to fail before he taught himself to win.

Think about where he started. A 300-pound man who couldn't swim well, trying to become a Navy SEAL. He knew he was going to fail again and again. In fact, it took him three attempts to get through SEAL training.

He says if you expect to win quickly, every failure feels like proof that you can't do it. If you expect failure as part of the path, it's just another step.

There's good research behind planning for this. Psychologist Peter Gollwitzer spent years studying simple "if, then" plans. If this happens, then I'll do that. A 2006 review that pulled together nearly a hundred studies found that people who made plans like this were clearly more likely to follow through on their goals than people who only set the goal.

The third thing Goggins does, he calls the cookie jar.

When things get hard, he reaches back into his memory for hard things he's already survived. The weight he lost. The exams he passed. The nights he trained alone. He says he did almost all of it with nobody watching and nobody clapping. So nobody can take that proof away from him.

That's where his second voice gets its power. It doesn't make things up. It uses evidence.

The last thing is the hardest. Goggins talks about the dark cupboards in your mind. The fears, failures, and old hurts you'd rather keep shut. He says he opens them every day and starts with the worst ones, because if he leaves them closed, they keep running his life from the inside.

You don't have to go as far as he does. But there's research here too. In the 1980s, psychologist James Pennebaker asked students to write about upsetting experiences for fifteen minutes a day, over four days. They felt worse right afterwards. But in the months that followed, they made fewer visits to the campus health centre than students who wrote about everyday topics.

Looking at the hard stuff on purpose seems to take away some of its grip.

Four tools you can start this week.

Tool one. Talk back with a fact. When the harsh voice says, you always quit, answer it out loud or on paper with something true. "I went to the gym twice last week." Every answer trains your second voice.

A simple way to start is two words: yes, and. "Yes, I used to quit. And this week I didn't." "Yes, I'm behind. And I did one page today." You're not arguing with the past. You're adding today's evidence to it.

Tool two. Fill your cookie jar. Tonight, write down ten hard things you've already got through in your life. Big or small. A tough exam, a breakup, a job you hated, a time you were scared and did it anyway. Read the list before anything hard. Small things count. Getting out of bed in a terrible week counts.

Tool three. Plan your failures. Pick one goal, and write down the three ways you'll most likely slip. Then write what you'll do when each one happens. "If I miss a morning, then I'll do ten minutes at lunch."

Tool four. Open one cupboard. Once this week, write for fifteen minutes about something you've been avoiding thinking about. Nobody has to read it. You can tear it up after.

One important note. If what's in your cupboards is heavy, like trauma or a voice that never lets up, please talk to a professional. Getting help is part of being strong.

One more thing to notice. In this series, the only person you compare yourself to is you, yesterday. Not Goggins. Not anyone on your feed. Your second voice gets stronger every time today's you does a little better than yesterday's.
