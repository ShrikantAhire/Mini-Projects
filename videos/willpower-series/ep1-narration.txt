At 24, David Goggins weighed nearly 300 pounds. He worked nights spraying restaurants for cockroaches. He stuttered, he could barely read, and at school there had been talk of putting him in a special class.

Years later he became a Navy SEAL, ran races longer than 200 miles, and set a world record with 4,030 pull-ups in one day.

People call him gifted. He says the opposite. He says he started at the bottom of the barrel, with no natural talent and a brain that struggles to hold on to what it learns.

This is episode one of a three-part series on how he built his willpower, and what you can use from it without living like him. Today: where willpower comes from, what science says about it, and the one daily habit that matters most.

Episode two is about the voices in your head. Episode three is about making it last. Watch them in order, because each one builds on the last.

Let's start with how he actually feels about the work.

When you watch clips of him, he looks fired up. Shouting, running, pushing. So people assume he runs on passion.

He says he hates running. He's called it the thing he dislikes most, and that's exactly why he does it. He doesn't wake up excited. He wakes up knowing the day will be hard, and he gets up anyway.

There are two ways to get a donkey moving. You can hold a carrot in front of it, or you can tap it from behind with a stick.

Most self-help is about carrots. Picture your dream body. Think about the reward. Find your passion.

Think about the last goal you set because of how good the finish line would feel. A fitter body. A promotion. A full savings account. How long did that picture keep you going on the days it rained?

Goggins says he runs almost entirely on the stick. A deep discomfort with the person he used to be, and a fear of sliding back into him. He gets a couple of minutes of satisfaction at the end of a hard day. By bedtime it's gone, and he's already checking what he got wrong.

You've probably noticed the carrot doesn't last. The fire you feel after a great video is gone by Wednesday. That's normal. Feelings rise and fall with your sleep, your mood, and your bank balance. Goggins stopped waiting for them.

He has a blunt way of putting it. When he weighed 300 pounds, there was no passion. When he couldn't read, there was no motivation. He just started, and kept going.

So is this something only he can do? He says no. Someone once told him on a podcast that he was blessed with a strong mind, and he pushed back hard. He says nobody handed it to him. He built it over decades, one hard day at a time, starting from the bottom. And science has some interesting things to say about that.

Deep in the middle of your brain, between the two halves, there's a small area scientists have been paying close attention to. It gets busy when you're deciding whether something is worth the effort, and when you push through something hard.

In 2016, a team at Massachusetts General Hospital studied people in their eighties who have the memory of someone thirty years younger. In these people, this area had held up, while in most people it thins with age.

Since then, other researchers have suggested this area may be central to tenacity, which simply means the ability to keep going when things get tough.

You'll see claims online that this part of the brain grows every time you do something you hate, and shrinks the moment you stop. It's a great story, and it fits Goggins perfectly. But it hasn't been proven. What scientists have so far are links, not proof that one causes the other. I'd rather tell you that than sell you a brain hack.

What is well established is about dopamine, the brain chemical most people link to pleasure.

In experiments by researcher John Salamone, rats with less dopamine in one key part of the brain stopped climbing over a small wall to reach better food. They settled for the easy food on the floor instead.

So dopamine is tied to how willing you are to work for a reward, as well as how good the reward feels. Your brain keeps track of effort, and that system responds to how you use it.

Goggins treats willpower like fitness. He calls it perishable. Stop training, and you lose it.

That's why, even after making a lot of money, he spends months each year as a wildland firefighter. He parachutes into remote fires, digs lines in the dirt, and breathes smoke. He turns down speaking money to do it. He says if he became someone who only gives talks, he'd lose the very thing that built him.

He also makes a point that surprises people. Once something hard starts to feel comfortable, it stops doing its job. If you love ice baths, ice baths aren't training your willpower. If you hate running, running is.

That brings us to the one habit that matters most. One hard thing, chosen on purpose, every day.

It has four simple rules.

Rule one. Pick something you really don't want to do. Something hard that you secretly enjoy doesn't count.

Rule two. Keep it small enough to do on your worst day. Five minutes is plenty to start.

Rule three. Do it at the same time each day, so it doesn't need a fresh decision every morning.

Rule four. When it starts to feel comfortable, swap it for something you're avoiding more.

Pause for a second. Think of one thing you've been avoiding for more than a week. That's probably your first hard thing.

Some ideas. Thirty seconds of cold water at the end of your shower. The phone call you've been putting off. Ten minutes on the hardest page of whatever you're studying. A walk when you'd rather scroll.

Tonight, write one line on a piece of paper. "Tomorrow, I'll do this, even though I don't want to." Fill in the blank, and put the paper where you'll see it in the morning.

In the first week, five minutes is enough. By week three, if it feels easier, stretch it to ten. You're not trying to suffer more each day. You're trying to keep showing up.

And if you miss a day, don't throw the whole thing away. Researchers in London who tracked people building new habits found that missing a single day didn't wreck their progress. The people who failed were the ones who quit. So if you miss, do the smallest version the next day and keep going.

Every time you do your hard thing, you also collect proof that you're someone who does hard things. Hold on to that idea. It becomes very important in episode two.

One honest warning. Goggins's life isn't balanced, and he says so himself. He sleeps very little, he trains through injuries, and he's been through heart surgery twice. You don't need his extremes.
