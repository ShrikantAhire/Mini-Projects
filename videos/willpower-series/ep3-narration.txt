David Goggins is in his late forties. He has money. He doesn't need to work another day in his life.

Yet he spends more than four hours a day studying medicine. For a test he's already passed.

This is the final episode of our series on how Goggins built his willpower. In episode one, we covered the stick, the carrot, and doing one hard thing on purpose every day. In episode two, we covered the two voices in your head and how to build the one that keeps you going. Both are linked in the description.

Today is about making it last. How he learns with a brain that struggles, how he protects his focus, how he plans his days, and how he keeps the people he loves while living this way.

Let's start with learning.

Goggins has talked about having ADHD, and about how hard it is for his brain to hold on to new information. He's watched his partner glance at a page and remember it, while he has to go over the same page again and again.

His method is simple and slow. He sits with a pen, paper, and his textbook, and he writes the same material out by hand, every day. He keeps going until he can picture the page in his head. In the exam, he flips through those pages in his mind to find the answer.

Then, once he knows the big picture, he goes back for the small details. First how to say a medicine's name. Then what it does. Then the dose. And the next day, he does it all again, because he says nothing ever stays learned for good.

Science backs a version of this. In 2006, researchers Henry Roediger and Jeffrey Karpicke compared two groups of students. One group read the material again and again. The other group read it, then closed the book and tried to recall it. A week later, the group that practised recalling remembered more.

Rereading feels easy, so it feels like learning. Pulling information out of your own head feels hard, and that's the version that sticks.

So if you're studying anything, close the book, write down everything you remember, then open it and check what you missed. That's the Goggins method, made simpler.

If you've ever felt slow at learning, take this from his story. He says he scored among the highest in the country on a paramedic exam, with a brain he describes as struggling more than most. Repetition did that.

Now focus.

Goggins says when he runs, he runs. He doesn't think about his studies. When he studies, he studies. Whatever he's doing gets all of him.

There's a reason that matters. In 2001, researchers Joshua Rubinstein, David Meyer and Jeffrey Evans studied what happens when people switch between tasks. Every switch cost time, and the cost grew as the tasks got more complicated. Jumping between your work and your phone feels quick, but it slows everything down.

Goggins also protects his time from other people. He does very few interviews. Someone else handles his messages. Some people find that rude. He says he can't think clearly about who he's becoming with a phone buzzing in his hand.

He compares it to being a painter. Every day he's painting a picture of himself, and a painter can't create while people keep talking to him.

Which leads to his planning. He says nothing in his day is made up on the fly. Before he sleeps, he already knows what tomorrow looks like. He spends time alone, without distractions, working out what comes next.

You can do a small version of this. Ten minutes every evening, phone in another room. Decide your one hard thing for tomorrow, and your one most important task. Write them down. When you wake up, the decision is already made.

During your most important task, put your phone in another room. Out of sight is easier than face down on the desk, because you're not spending energy ignoring it.

One balance point. Goggins himself says sleep, food and recovery matter. He admits he sometimes trades them away to chase his goals. You don't need to. A tired brain makes every hard thing harder.

And when the time comes, start. Even if it's 12:33 and you were planning to start at the top of the hour. More on that in the next video.

Now, relationships. This is the part of Goggins's life people ask about least, and it's one of the most useful.

He admits his life is unbalanced. Early mornings, late nights, long stretches alone. So he does two things.

First, he makes sure the people in his family have what they need. His time, his support, his love. He doesn't skip that part.

Second, he's completely upfront about what he needs in return. Time alone to train, study, and think. He tells people this at the start, so nobody is surprised later.

He says one of his biggest mistakes, earlier in life, was trying to please everyone. The people around him were happy, and he felt empty. He says that isn't a real relationship.

You don't need to live like him to use this. If you're changing your life, the people close to you deserve to know why. One clear sentence is enough. "This matters to me. I'll be training from six to seven, and after that I'm all yours."

Saying it early prevents a lot of resentment later, on both sides. And ask them what they need from you too. It works best as a two-way deal.

Then there are the critics. Goggins gets called crazy a lot. His view is that when someone can't picture themselves doing something, the person doing it looks crazy to them.

What protects him is something he calls the unseen work. Almost everything he did, he did alone. Running in the middle of the night. Losing the weight in a cold Indiana winter. Studying the same page for hours. Nobody clapped. So when people judge him, he has proof they can't see, and that proof is enough.

When someone calls your new habit extreme, remember they're seeing one moment. They didn't see the weeks behind it.

You build the same thing every time you do your one hard thing when nobody's watching. It goes straight into your cookie jar.

The last idea in this series is the simplest. Goggins says he's never finished.

He's had multiple knee surgeries and two heart surgeries. Doctors have told him more than once he'd never run again. He still runs. He says when the day comes that he truly can't, he'll find another hard thing, because it was never about running.

Willpower stays only as long as you keep using it. When your hard thing becomes easy, that's your sign to pick a new one, just like in episode one.
